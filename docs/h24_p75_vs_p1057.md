# n=24 halver: candidate `p75` vs the committed best `p1057` — head-to-head

**TL;DR.** Both are valid **80-comparator** split@12 halvers for n=24. **Which one is
faster is architecture-dependent.** `p75` executes *fewer instructions on every
element type*, and on this box (Intel Meteor-Lake P-core) it is faster on `i64`
(≈tie) and `pair64f` (−4%) but **12% slower on `pair64` (lex)** — because its
comparator ordering packs denser data-dependencies and the lexicographic
compare-exchange's latency lands on the critical path, dropping IPC from 4.30 to
3.81. On µarchs that hide those stalls (bigger OoO window / cheaper pair compare),
`p75`'s lower instruction count wins outright — which matches the report that **`p75`
is faster on other CPUs**. On *this* core the `pair64` loss outweighs the small wins,
so `nets::h24` stays at `p1057` here; the choice should be revisited per deployment
µarch.

## The two networks
| net | CE | depth | status |
|-----|----|-------|--------|
| `p1057` | 80 | 18 | committed `nets::h24` (fastest on this Meteor-Lake P-core) |
| `p75`   | 80 | 17 | pool candidate (`seeds/h24_80pool.txt`); starts `{14,15},{20,22},{12,13},{3,6},{16,17},{0,23},…` |

Both verified valid split@12 via the C(24,12)=2,704,156 k-zero sweep.

## Methodology — reliable (cycle-accurate) benchmark
The earlier ns/wall-clock numbers swing with turbo and are contaminated by the
per-rep buffer copy. The halver kernels are **fully branchless** (cmov
compare-exchange, **zero conditional branches** — confirmed in the disassembly), so
the retired-instruction stream and cycle count are **data-independent**. The
reliable harness (`optimizers/bench_cycles.{cpp,sh}`) exploits that:
- no per-rep copy — hammer the kernel on one L1-resident batch (96 blocks);
- measure with `perf` HW counters on a pinned P-core (`cpu_core/cycles/`,
  `cpu_core/instructions/`), **median of 5 runs**, 30 000 reps each;
- report **cycles/element** (frequency-invariant — not ns), **IPC** (reveals the
  *mechanism*), and **instructions/element** (the portable "work" metric).

`optimizers/bench_cycles.sh 24 netA.txt netB.txt` reproduces everything below.

## Cycle-accurate results (median of 5 perf runs; frequency-invariant)
| type | `p1057` cyc/el | `p75` cyc/el | p75 vs p1057 | `p1057` IPC | `p75` IPC | `p1057` ins/el | `p75` ins/el |
|------|---------------:|-------------:|:------------:|------------:|----------:|---------------:|-------------:|
| i64           | 3.48 | 3.49 | **≈0 %** (±1) | 5.35 | 5.22 | 18.58 | **18.21** |
| pair64 (lex)  | 15.07 | 16.89 | **+12 %** | **4.30** | **3.81** | 64.70 | **64.15** |
| pair64f (first-key) | 7.49 | 7.23 | **−4 %** | 5.86 | 5.88 | 43.97 | **42.29** |

Legacy ns benchmark (`bench_pool`, median of 25 runs) agreed: pair64 +12.7 %, sum
+6.7 % — but cycles/IPC are the trustworthy figures.

Two facts to hold together:
1. **`p75` does strictly less work** — fewer instructions/element on *all three*
   types.
2. **Yet on `pair64` it costs 12 % more cycles**, because its **IPC collapses
   4.30 → 3.81 (−11 %)**. Fewer instructions, more cycles ⇒ the bottleneck is
   pipeline stalls (dependencies), not work.

## Root cause — comparator ordering / dependency density
Static disassembly (per 24-element block, `apply_network`, `-O3 -march=native`,
`no-tree-vectorize`) is essentially identical — for `pair64`: same 320 `cmov`, 240
`cmp`, **0** branches, and `p75` even issues 8 fewer instructions and 22 fewer
memory reads. So the difference is dynamic. The discriminating static metric is the
**write-distance** of each comparator (its index minus the last index that wrote
either of its wires; small = tight serialising dependency):

| net | dist=1 (back-to-back) | dist≤2 | mean | median |
|-----|---------------------:|-------:|-----:|-------:|
| `p1057` | 11 | **16** | 6.46 | 5 |
| `p75`   | 14 | **27** | 6.29 | 4 |

`p75` has **27** compare-exchanges (vs 16) whose inputs were produced ≤2 steps
earlier. A `pair64` lex compare-exchange is a multi-cycle dependency (compare
`.first`, compare `.second`, combine masks, conditionally move two 8-byte fields);
when the next comparator consumes a just-written wire, that latency serialises and
the OoO core cannot overlap it — exactly the IPC drop measured. The cheap paths are
immune: `i64` is a 1-cycle `cmp`+`cmov`, and `pair64f` compares only the single
projected key (80 `cmp`, not 240) — short dependencies, so `p75`'s lower
instruction count makes it the *faster* net there.

Note the depths run against intuition: `p75` is **shallower** (17 vs 18) yet slower
on pair64. Comparator-network "depth" (parallel layers) is not the scheduled
critical path of the generated kernel; under a throughput workload the per-block
dependency interleave — a property of the **emission order** — is what matters.

## Why `p75` wins on other architectures
`p75`'s advantage (fewer instructions on every type) is portable; its disadvantage
(low `pair64` IPC) is specific to how *this* core schedules the lex
compare-exchange's dependency chains. On a µarch with a deeper reorder buffer,
different `cmov`/compare latencies, or a cheaper 16-byte conditional move, the
`pair64` stalls shrink or vanish and the lower instruction count dominates → `p75`
becomes the faster net overall. This is the expected outcome of selecting halvers by
on-device measurement: **among equal-CE nets the winner is µarch-specific**, so a net
that loses here can win elsewhere.

## Conclusion
- On this **Intel Meteor-Lake P-core**, `p1057` is faster overall (the `pair64`
  +12 % outweighs the `i64`/`pair64f` wins); `nets::h24` stays `p1057`.
- `p75` is a strong portable alternative — fewer instructions everywhere — and is
  expected to win on µarchs less sensitive to the `pair64` dependency stalls
  (consistent with it being faster on other CPUs). If the deployment target weights
  `pair64`/lex less, or is such a µarch, prefer `p75`.
- **Process improvement:** halver selection should use the cycle-accurate harness
  (`optimizers/bench_cycles.sh`: perf cycles/IPC, branchless ⇒ no-copy, median of N)
  rather than ns timing, and ideally be confirmed on each target µarch. The
  write-distance-≤2 count is a cheap static predictor of dependency-bound slowness
  to glance at when shortlisting.
